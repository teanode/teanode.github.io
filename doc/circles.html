Circles and chats

A server usually has people on it who know each other: a household, a few friends, a team. A circle says who belongs together. On its own it shares nothing; it is the list that chats, calendars, albums, memory, skills and location ask when somebody wants to share with "my family".

Everyone keeps their own account, their own mailbox and their own agent. A circle never lets one person read another's mail or conversations, an operator included. What crosses between people is only what its owner chose to share, and leaving a circle ends every share that depended on it at once.

Making a circle

Open Circles from the account menu at the foot of the rail, or in the iPhone app's side menu, and make one with a name and a kind: family, friends or colleagues. The kind is only how screens describe it.

  • Somebody with an account is invited by username, and joins when they accept. The invitation waits on their Circles page, in their agent's conversation and on their phone, and expires after thirty days.
  • Somebody with no account yet is added with Make an account for someone, by an operator: a username, a name and a first password, and they are in the circle at once.

Each member is an admin or a member. Admins rename the circle, invite and remove people, and decide who else is an admin; a circle always keeps one. Anybody may leave. A role never decides what anybody sees of anybody else: that is always the owner's choice.

From the command line:

teanode circle create --kind family Family
teanode circle invite <circle-id> sam
teanode user create --circle <circle-id> alex

Chats

Once two people share a circle they can message each other. Chats live in the same drawer as your agent, and in the iPhone app's Chats: start one with New conversation, then Message someone or New group. Every circle also has a chat of its own, whose members follow the circle's.

A chat works with no model at all: messages, pictures and files, replies, reactions, read marks and notifications on the phone. Agents join only when somebody asks:

  • Your own agent answers in a chat when you mention it with @ or reply to something it said. What it reads and does there is shown only to you.
  • Somebody else's agent answers you only if its owner allowed it in that chat, and then only from the chat and the web, never from its owner's mail, calendar or memory.

Sharing

Each kind of thing is shared with one person in a circle, or with a whole circle, at a level its owner picks.

  • Calendars, at busy, titles, details or edit. A circle can also keep a calendar of its own, and Find a time looks across everyone's. An event marked private stays busy whatever the level.
  • Albums, to look at or to add to, with or without where the photos were taken. See Photos.
  • Where you are, now or now and where you have been. See Location.
  • Pages of memory, read only, with or without the words each fact came from.
  • A skill that costs money, with a limit per payment and per month; anything above the limit waits for the owner, who approves it on the dashboard or in the iPhone app.

Who is who

In a circle you can say what somebody is to you: Sam is my father. Sam sees the other side at once (you are Sam's son, daughter or child) and is told. Either of you can change it or take it away, and only the two of you see it. On your profile you can say how you are described, he, she or they; until you do, the words for you are neutral.

Your agent knows these, so "ask my father whether Sunday works" or "where is my wife" reaches the right account, and it never guesses a relationship or a pronoun. On the command line they are teanode circle relate, teanode circle unrelate and teanode user update --pronoun.

Documentation

Getting started

  • What is TeaNode: Send and receive mail at your own domains: mailboxes read here or over IMAP, forwarding, what it does, and what you need to run it.
  • Quick start: TeaNode running with docker compose in a few minutes: the assistant first, with no domain, then mail at your own domain if you want it.
  • Getting started: Standing up a mail server for one domain, from nothing to mail arriving in a mailbox you can read in a mail program, including the DNS records and the port 25 problem.

Running it

  • Deploying: The container image and the compose file, what runs beside the server, how it upgrades itself, and what to back up.
  • Configuration: Where the settings live, the environment variables that say how to reach them, and every field an operator can set.
  • Agents: The personal agent: what it needs, what it does with a mailbox, and what to weigh before turning it on.
  • Memory: What your agent keeps between conversations: a graph of pages it writes without being asked, the places it reads from, and what it does with all of it in its dreams.
  • Voice: Call your agent from the chat drawer: say what you want, hear the answer as it is written, and cut in when you like. On a laptop or a phone.
  • Coding sessions: Your Claude Code and Codex sessions in herdr, on every computer you attached: your agent reads them, types into them where you can see, and brings you their questions to answer from anywhere.
  • Memory in coding sessions: Claude Code and Codex shown what your agent knows about the checkout they run in: the project page and the last session when one starts, recall kept to the project before each prompt, and the transcript read within a minute.
  • Model Context Protocol: TeaNode on both ends of the protocol: answering it, so your editor can use your agent's tools and ask it questions, and connecting out to servers of your own.
  • Finance: Your banks, cards and brokerages linked through Plaid or SimpleFIN: transactions in your own categories, budgets that say where the month is heading, savings targets, net worth, and a tax year put together from your paystubs, W-2s, 1099s and 1098s, all kept in your own database.
  • Circles and chats: The people on one server in circles: chats with each other in the drawer and the app, with or without their agents, and calendars, albums, memory pages, skills and location shared at the level each owner picks.
  • Photos: Photos and videos on your own server: backed up from the iPhone in the background, found by place, date or what is in them, faces grouped into people you name, and albums shared with your circle.
  • Location: Visits to named places recorded by the iPhone app, a map of your day, where you are shared with the people in your circle, and what leaves the server to name a place.
  • The command line: The two programs: the server and the few things only its host can do, and the client that administers it from anywhere through the API.

Security

  • Security review: A review of the whole program: what was examined, what was found, what was fixed, and what is still open.

Contributing
